To give you a bit more context before the call, let me be more precise about your role than my previous email was.

The question is not *whether* the epigenetic clock ticks under hTERT and hypoxia. That is already known: Kabacik, Horvath & Raj (2018, Aging) showed that hTERT expression does not arrest epigenetic aging, and Matsuyama, Horvath et al. (2019, Aging) showed that hypoxia slows but does not stop it. Your co-author Horvath already answered the "whether" question. We do not need to repeat that.

What we need to measure — and what no one has measured — is **which clock reaches the finish line first, and under what conditions.**

Here is the experimental logic:

We use BJ-hTERT human fibroblasts at 2% O₂. Active telomerase protects telomeres (C5). Low oxygen protects mitochondria (C3). Two counters are protected. Three remain in the race: C1 (centriolar polyE accumulation), C2 (DNA methylation clock), C4 (structural — centriole/primary cilium dysmorphometry). But C4 is likely downstream of C1 — the tubulin code degrades as polyE accumulates.

So the true race is between C1 and C2.

Your role (C2): Illumina EPIC arrays at three passage points — P25, P35, P45 — and at replicative arrest. From the same cells. At the same time points, we measure C1: polyE levels on the mother centriole (U-ExM, Magiera lab) and division count (ARGUS-LP live-cell tracking, Jacquemet lab).

The question: when the cell finally stops dividing, is it because (a) the centriole reached its polyE threshold, while the epigenetic clock still had room to tick, or (b) the epigenetic clock hit its critical methylation state first, while the centriole was still functional?

Your clock is not just the temporal reference. It is the competitor. We are staging a race between two molecular aging clocks on the same cells, and your clock is one of the runners.
