Dear Mónica,

I am writing because I think there is an experiment your lab is uniquely positioned to do, and because Pierre Gönczy confirmed to me last week that no one has ever done it.

**The question:** If you eliminate centrioles from a somatic fibroblast and then attempt reprogramming, what happens?

CEDAR — a theory I published in 2023 (PMID 36583780) — argues that the centriole is the cell's hardware. Unlike the epigenome, which OSKM factors can reset, the centriole accumulates polyglutamylation over time and cannot be repaired in situ. It does not simply block reprogramming — it carries a directional arrow. The age and state of the centriole tells the cell where it sits on the differentiation trajectory and which way it is headed across generations.

The prediction: if the centriole provides the directional signal that keeps a cell on its somatic trajectory, removing it should lift that constraint. Reprogramming should become more efficient — not because a barrier was removed, but because the arrow pointing toward the somatic fate is gone.

**This experiment has never been published.** Pierre Gönczy wrote to me on 9 July: "I am not aware of this either." His lab works on C. elegans centriole elimination and declined to participate. The question is wide open.

I have attached a one-page concept note with the experimental logic, controls, and a practical detail that may have hidden this result until now — Uetake et al. (2007) showed that acentriolar normal cells arrest through p38, not p53. Most reprogramming protocols only block p53. Without SB203580, the cells never get a chance to show the effect.

Your 2016 Science paper on centriole elimination in Drosophila oocytes (PMID 27229142) is one of the key references in our framework. If this is something you would consider discussing, even briefly, I would be grateful.

We are theorists. We have the logic and the design. We need a bench partner who knows centrioles.
