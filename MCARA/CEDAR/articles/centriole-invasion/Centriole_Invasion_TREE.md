# The Centriole Invasion

## How a Phage Tubulin Gave Rise to Irreversible Differentiation

**Author:** Jaba Tqemaladze
**Affiliation:** Georgia Longevity Alliance

---

## Abstract

The standard view holds that the Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor (LECA) possessed centrioles, and that land plants secondarily lost them. I explore an alternative: (1) the first cells — bacteria — were walled and acentriolar; architecturally they were «plant-like» (a heuristic analogy, not a homology), regardless of where life originated; (2) in permanent water, some lineages shed the wall, giving rise to Amoebozoa with acentriolar centrosomes, exemplified by *Dictyostelium*; (3) the phage tubulin PhuZ shows that dynamic tubulin spindles predate eukaryotes — no horizontal gene transfer was needed, only regulatory innovation; (4) the centriole became a cell-fate tag: polyglutamylation (counterbalanced by CCP deglutamylases) and asymmetric inheritance biased cell-fate decisions; (5) true animal tissues — with irreversibly differentiated cells, distinct from the modulated, totipotent cells of bacterial colonies, fungal hyphae, and plant meristems — thus emerged; (6) aging is the gradual failure of this tagging system. The LECA consensus favours an ancestral centriole; the phage-centriole link remains speculative; deglutamylases render polyGlu reversible. Three testable predictions are provided. Five alternative models are evaluated.

**Keywords:** centriole evolution; phage tubulin PhuZ; cell wall; UNIMOD; polyglutamylation; asymmetric centriole inheritance; irreversible differentiation; CEDAR; *Dictyostelium*; stem cell aging

## 1. The Argument in Brief

1. **Cell wall = external skeleton. Centriole = internal skeleton.** A cell cannot have both. Bacteria came first, on land, where a wall is essential. In the sea, the wall was shed. Its loss created pressure for an internal skeleton — the centriole.
2. **PhuZ shows the tubulin fold can form a spindle.** No HGT required — only regulatory innovation.
3. **The centriole consolidates epigenetic differentiation, making it irreversible.**
4. **Aging is the decay of this system.**

## 2. Bacteria: The First Cells Were Walled and Acentriolar

By «life» we mean cellular life — bacteria, not viruses. The geological record is unambiguous on two points. **First, bacteria are far older than animals.** The oldest fossil evidence of life dates to ~3.5 Ga (Pilbara Craton, Dresser Formation [2]), while the first animals appear ~0.6 Ga (Ediacaran). Bacteria dominated Earth for ~85% of its history. **Second, all bacteria possess a rigid cell wall (peptidoglycan). No bacterium possesses a centriole.** Architecturally, the first cells were «plant-like»: walled, acentriolar, heterotrophic — consuming abiotically produced organic molecules or, later, photosynthesising [6]. Whether the earliest bacteria lived in terrestrial hot springs [2], shallow marine waters [3], or deep-sea vents [1] is debated — but this does not affect the architectural fact: **the ancestral cellular architecture was walled and acentriolar.**


**Archaeal walls — different chemistry, same architectural function.** Archaea possess S-layer or pseudopeptidoglycan walls, chemically distinct from bacterial peptidoglycan but serving the identical architectural role: an external skeleton. Asgard archaea — the closest known prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes — are walled and acentriolar. This reinforces the rule across domains.

**Bacteriophage or archaeophage?** The eukaryotic host is of archaeal origin (Asgard). Two viral routes to centriole evolution must therefore be considered:

**(i) Bacteriophage route (PhuZ paradigm).** PhuZ from Pseudomonas phages forms a dynamic 3-filament bipolar spindle positioning viral DNA [16,17,18]. PhuZ has <11% identity with α/β-tubulin and lacks the H6 helix. It demonstrates tubulin fold evolvability but cannot be a direct ancestor: bacteriophages cannot infect archaeal hosts — the peptidoglycan wall chemistry and receptor systems are incompatible with the archaeal S-layer. For a bacteriophage to contribute to centriole evolution, a gene would need to cross domains via an intermediate host — possible, but unparsimonious.

**(ii) Archaeophage route — more parsimonious.** Archaeal viruses infect archaeal hosts directly. They exhibit extraordinary morphological diversity: spindle-shaped (Sulfolobus spindle-shaped viruses, SSV), bottle-shaped (Ampullaviridae), lemon-shaped (Fuselloviridae), and two-tailed (Acidianus two-tailed virus, ATV). Some archaeal viruses encode cytoskeletal proteins. If any viral contribution to centriole evolution occurred, the **archaeophage scenario is favoured by parsimony:** an archaeal virus infecting the Asgard-like host directly, without crossing domains. The virus could have contributed either a tubulin-like gene or a self-assembling compartment principle — both demonstrated by different archaeal virus families.

**(iii) Neither — regulatory innovation suffices.** The host Asgard archaeon already possessed FtsZ/tubulin homologs [48]. SAS-6, PLK4, and CEP135 are eukaryotic innovations with no known viral homologs. No viral contribution is required. PhuZ — whether from bacteriophage or archaeophage — serves as a **paradigm** demonstrating that dynamic tubulin-based spindles are evolvable, not as evidence for a specific HGT event.

## 3. Into Water: Wall Loss and the Amoeboid Intermediate

From whatever environment they originated, bacteria colonised permanent water bodies. In constant water, the cell wall became dispensable — and a barrier to phagocytosis and motility. Some lineages shed it.

The Amoebozoa represent the wall-less, acentriolar state. *Dictyostelium discoideum* [7] possesses a centrosome — layered core + γ-tubulin corona — but **no centrioles** [8–13].

**The LECA consensus — reframed through UNIMOD.** Gräf et al. (2015) state: «LECA most probably already possessed one or two basal bodies» [10b]. The presence of centriolar gene homologs (SAS-6, PLK4, CEP135, γ-tubulin) in all five eukaryotic supergroups strongly supports this. However, Carvalho-Santos et al. (2010) proposed the UNIMOD model [45]: LECA possessed a universal module of centriolar proteins that could assemble either into a centriole OR function as an acentriolar MTOC, depending on ecological context. Under UNIMOD, *Dictyostelium* and land plants are not «secondary losses» but **facultatively acentriolar** — they possess the toolkit but do not deploy it for centriole assembly in somatic cells. Animals constitutively deploy it. This reconciles the phylogenomic data (genes present in all supergroups) with the architectural observation (structures absent in many lineages) without requiring either universal LECA centrioles or multiple independent origins. It also explains why plants can assemble up to 50,000 centrioles de novo in sperm cells (blepharoplast) — the toolkit is present, just suppressed in somatic tissue.

**How does an acentriolar centrosome build a spindle? Centromere clustering and the nuclear tether.** The most complete mechanistic account of acentriolar spindle organization comes from Gräf, Grafe and Meyer (2026) [49]. In *Dictyostelium*, the centromeres of all six chromosomes cluster in a Rabl-like configuration at the nuclear envelope [49,51] and are physically tethered to the centrosome through a LINC-type bridge: the SUN-domain protein Sun-1 spans the nuclear envelope and connects the cytosolic centrosome to the lamin NE81 and to the centromere cluster [31,50]. At the G2→M transition, the centrosome penetrates a fenestra in the persisting nuclear envelope and duplicates; microtubules then connect the two mitotic centrosomes to the kinetochores, assembling a functional bipolar spindle [49]. The centromere protein Cenp68 — related to fission-yeast monopolin — is proposed to couple the clustered centromeres to the spindle and accelerate spindle assembly [49]. Four conclusions follow. **First**, an acentriolar centrosome can organize a complete, kinetochore-connected spindle: centrioles are dispensable for spindle mechanics — in *Dictyostelium* and in vertebrate cells, where the PCNT–CDK5RAP2 pericentriolar matrix suffices for centriole-independent spindle assembly [55]. **Second**, centromere clustering plus the nuclear tether constitute a centriole-independent system of positional memory — the acentriolar counterpart of the centriolar fate tag (see §5). **Third**, this reinforces the core argument of the present essay: the evolutionary innovation of the centriole was not spindle mechanics (which demonstrably predates it) but **fate tagging**. **Fourth**, the acentriolar state is not an exotic anomaly — it is a complete, stable, inheritable cellular architecture, and reversible centriole depletion in vertebrate cells [33] plus centriole-independent spindle assembly [55] bear directly on the feasibility of acentriolar windows in reprogramming contexts.

## 4. Phages: An Illustration of Pre-Eukaryotic Tubulin Dynamics

**Note on HGT and the wall.** If centriolar genes were acquired by HGT, the bacterial cell wall is not a barrier — conjugation, transduction, and transformation readily transfer DNA across walls. The wall argument applies only to a fully formed centriole physically entering a cell, which is not proposed.

**The centriole has two modes — both absent in plants.** In animal cells, the centriole operates in two distinct functional modes: **(i) signalling mode** — as a basal body organising the cilium, a sensory and signalling organelle (Hedgehog, Wnt, TGF-β); and **(ii) MTOC mode** — as the core of the centrosome, recruiting the pericentriolar matrix (PCM: CDK5RAP2, CEP192, pericentrin, γ-tubulin ring complexes) to nucleate and organise cytoplasmic microtubules and the mitotic spindle. In land plant somatic cells, **neither mode operates.** Plants lack cilia entirely [5b] and organise microtubules through dispersed, transient nucleation sites — the preprophase band, the phragmoplast, and the cortical array — using γ-tubulin but without a permanent PCM scaffold. Plant centriolar gene homologs (SAS-6, PLK4, CEP135) are present in *Arabidopsis* but are **co-opted for acentrosomal functions**, not centriole assembly. Only in the male gametes of basal land plants (mosses, ferns, cycads, ginkgo) do centrioles form **de novo** — in ginkgo and cycads, the blepharoplast assembles up to **50,000 centrioles per cell** [5c], which organise the mitotic spindle and then become basal bodies for the sperm flagellum. After fertilisation, they are eliminated. This is a natural experiment in centriole regulation: the plant genome retains the full assembly program but **suppresses it in somatic cells** while activating it in germ cells. The animal lineage, by contrast, **constitutively activates** the program. This supports the tag model: the difference between plants and animals is not genetic potential but regulatory architecture.

This dual-mode architecture — cilium + centrosome — is the key animal innovation. It is the PCM, more than the centriole itself, that constitutes the functional MTOC. *Dictyostelium* demonstrates this: it possesses a permanent PCM-based centrosome (the NAB — nucleus-associated body; whether NAB is strictly homologous to the animal centrosome or an independently evolved MTOC remains debated [7,8]) The NAB is a PCM without a centriole; the animal centrosome is a PCM with a centriole. The centriole added cilium-organising capacity to a pre-existing PCM-based MTOC. The comprehensive 2026 account of acentriolar spindle organization in *Dictyostelium* — centromere clustering, nuclear-envelope fenestration, and kinetochore–centrosome connections [49] — closes the mechanistic gap: an acentriolar centrosome builds a complete bipolar spindle.

**Plants have a different system entirely.** Instead of a centriole and a permanent PCM-based centrosome, land plant somatic cells use a **distributed, chromosome-directed microtubule organising system:** (i) the **preprophase band** (PPB) — a cortical ring of microtubules and actin that marks the future division plane before mitosis; (ii) the **phragmoplast** — a microtubule array that builds the new cell wall during cytokinesis; (iii) **cortical microtubules** — a dispersed array controlling cell shape; and (iv) the **Ran-GTP gradient** emanating from chromosomes, which organises the acentrosomal mitotic spindle. This system is not a «degenerate» centrosome — it is an independently evolved, position-based architecture for microtubule organisation. The plant cell does not need a centriole because it has the PPB-phragmoplast-RanGTP system. The animal cell needs the centriole/PCM because it lacks a cell wall and must organise its cytoplasm from within.

**PhuZ: convergent evolution of the tubulin spindle.** PhuZ shares <11% sequence identity with α/β-tubulin, assembles into 3-filament structures, and is phylogenetically closer to FtsZ [16,17]. It is not an ancestor — it is an independent reinvention. PhuZ demonstrates that the tubulin fold is **evolutionarily evolvable:** a protein with minimal sequence similarity can converge on the same function — a dynamic, bipolar spindle. This functional parallelism removes any need to postulate HGT.

**Why the 3→9 filament gap is not a problem.** PhuZ does not need to become a centriole. The eukaryotic host already possessed α/β-tubulin, which forms 13-filament microtubules. The centriole's 9-fold symmetry is imposed by the **SAS-6 cartwheel** — a protein scaffold that templates nine microtubule triplets, independently of which tubulin fills them. The evolutionary innovation is not the tubulin, but the cartwheel. PhuZ simply demonstrates that a tubulin fold protein can form a dynamic spindle — a proof of principle that the fold was evolvable long before SAS-6 organised it into 9-fold symmetry.

**Alternative models** — autogenous origin [22], spirochete endosymbiosis [23], viral eukaryogenesis [24] — are evaluated in §9.

## 5. The Centriole: From Reversible Modulation to Irreversible Differentiation

**The centriole did not invent differentiation.** Epigenetic mechanisms — DNA methylation, histone modifications, transcriptional networks (the Waddington landscape) — evolved independently and are sufficient for cell specialisation. Plants, fungi, and even bacterial biofilms exhibit functional specialisation without centrioles. But this specialisation is **modulation** — reversible under physiological conditions. A plant mesophyll cell can dedifferentiate and regenerate an entire organism via somatic embryogenesis.

**The centriole determines cell fate.** Extensive correlative evidence — satisfying 8 of 9 Bradford-Hill criteria in the author's meta-analysis [40b] — supports the centriole as a key component of the cell-fate machinery, though definitive proof (fate rescue by centriole transplantation) is lacking. The evidence spans model systems: (i) *Drosophila* male GSCs — mother centrosome retained in stem daughter, daughter centrosome enters differentiating gonialblast [26]; (ii) *Drosophila* neuroblasts — daughter centriole retained in stem cell through Cnn-mediated PCM asymmetry [27]; (iii) mammalian neural progenitors — mother-centriole retention maintains stemness [28]; (iv) CD8+ T cells — daughter receiving mother centrosome becomes memory cell, the other becomes effector [29]; (v) laser ablation of centrioles alters daughter cell fate [32]; (vi) PLK4 inhibition reversibly removes centrioles and alters differentiation [33]. The direction of inheritance varies between tissues (neuroblasts retain the daughter centriole; GSCs and mammalian neural progenitors retain the mother) — but **asymmetry itself is the biological fact.** Tissue-specific polarity does not weaken the model; it demonstrates that centriole identity — whichever centriole is retained — correlates with and determines somatic cell fate. The centriole = fate of the somatic animal cell.

By adding a cytoplasmic layer of cell-fate tagging — polyglutamylation, asymmetric inheritance, and the combinatorial PTM code (CAMC) — the centriole **consolidated** the epigenetic differentiation system and **locked** it. The epigenetic machinery specifies *which* genes are active; the centriole specifies that this state is *permanent* for the lifetime of the cell. This is the evolutionary transition from **reversible modulation to irreversible differentiation.**

**(a) C1 — polyglutamylation: a molecular tag, not a lock.** [As before.] TTLL enzymes add polyGlu chains to centriolar tubulin; CCP deglutamylases remove them [25,26a]. The steady-state level reflects a **dynamic equilibrium.** CSAP binds specifically to polyglutamylated microtubules [34,35]; loss of polyGlu abolishes CSAP localization. PolyGlu is thus a **molecular tag** — an instruction for recruiting specific MAPs (microtubule-associated proteins) — not an irreversible lock. Different tag patterns (chain length, branching, position along the centriole) recruit different effector proteins, creating a **combinatorial state marker** that reflects the centriole's history.

**Aging is the difference in time since formation.** The centriole, like all material structures, accumulates entropy: protein oxidation, conformational defects, wear on the 9-fold symmetry. This is **centriolar aging** — a physical process, not a regulatory one. **Caveat:** centriolar proteins undergo turnover (chaperones, ubiquitin-proteasome system), and the extent to which this mitigates entropy accumulation is unknown. Polyglutamylation is the cell's **compensatory response:** by tagging older centrioles, the cell recruits stabilising proteins (CSAP, MAPs) that counteract structural degradation. The tag does not cause aging — it **marks** it, and attempts to **compensate** for it. When compensation fails, the centriole's function degrades.

**Crucial boundary condition — CCP5 knockout and the germline reset.** Giordano et al. (2019) showed that CCP5−/− mice accumulate hyperglutamylated tubulin and develop **supernumerary centrioles** specifically in spermatids [36] — not in somatic cells. This tissue-specificity is instructive: spermatids are precisely the cells in which centrioles are being **transformed** for transmission to the next generation. In normal fertilisation, the sperm contributes a modified centriole that serves as a **seed** (затравка), not a template (матрица), for de novo centriole assembly in blastomeres. The oocyte **eliminates its centrioles entirely,** ensuring the new organism starts with zero accumulated parental deformations. The CCP5−/− phenotype thus reveals not a generic failure of tagging, but a **disruption of the germline reset mechanism** — the process by which centriolar age and entropy are erased between generations. This is precisely what the CEDAR model predicts: if centrioles accumulate entropy over time, sexual reproduction must actively reset this entropy. CCP5 is part of that reset machinery.

**How does a reversible tag mediate irreversible differentiation?** PolyGlu is reversible — it cannot, by itself, be an irreversible lock. Three complementary mechanisms are proposed:

**(i) DID — Determinants of Irreversible Differentiation.** During de novo centriole assembly, RNA/protein complexes are deposited within the centriolar lumen. These complexes — DIDs — are physically sequestered within the centriole. During asymmetric division, the centriole inherited by the differentiating daughter releases one or more DIDs into the cytoplasm, where they translocate to the nucleus and remodel chromatin, closing pluripotency networks and opening lineage-specific ones. The irreversibility resides in the chromatin state. The centriole is a **dispenser** of pre-assembled determinants.

**(ii) Cartwheel deformation.** The SAS-6 cartwheel — the 9-fold symmetric core of the centriole — undergoes structural deformation over successive cell cycles. This deformation is transmitted to daughter centrioles during duplication and can be detected by downstream effectors. Experimental evidence demonstrates that cartwheel geometry changes with centriole age. Cartwheel strain may directly influence the recruitment of PCM components and, through them, gene expression programmes.

**(iii) LINC-lamin mechanotransduction.** PolyGlu recruits CSAP, altering PCM architecture. PCM remodelling changes mechanical tension on the LINC complex, which physically connects the centrosome to the nuclear envelope. Altered LINC tension is transmitted to nuclear lamins, repositioning lamina-associated chromatin domains. This pathway — polyGlu → CSAP → PCM → LINC → lamins → chromatin — makes the centriole a **mechanotransduction element.**

**Centriole number dysregulation — a nuanced picture.** Centrosome amplification (CA) is a hallmark of cancer [37], but the relationship is not binary. CA occurs physiologically in healthy hepatocytes, neuronal progenitors, megakaryocytes, and immune cells, where it can enhance migration and effector functions. Experimental CA induction (PLK4 overexpression) on a mutant p53 background drives tumorigenesis; without p53 loss, CA causes severe fitness defects incompatible with transformation. **Multinucleated cells** — a common result of cytokinetic failure — frequently contain amplified, inactivated centrioles and represent an alternative fate to malignancy. **Cells with inactivated centrioles** (centriole loss through centrinone, or natural elimination as in *C. elegans*) can continue to divide, demonstrating that centriole number per se is not the determinant — it is the **combination of CA with p53 surveillance failure** that enables transformation. The model thus predicts three regimes plus a boundary condition: (i) **physiological polyGlu** — moderate tagging, CSAP recruitment, correct asymmetric division; (ii) **insufficient tagging** — structural degradation, stem cell exhaustion; (iii) **pathological hyper-tagging + p53 loss** — centriole amplification, genomic instability, cancer; (iv) **CA without p53 loss** — fitness defects, multinucleation, or centriole inactivation, incompatible with transformation.

**(b) C2 — asymmetric inheritance biases cell fate.** [Unchanged.]

**(c) CAMC — a combinatorial state marker.** CAMC is redefined as the **combinatorial PTM code** of the centriole — the specific pattern of polyglutamylation, acetylation (Lys40), and associated proteins (Ninein, CEP170, ODF2, CSAP) that collectively constitute a **molecular tag.** Different tag patterns recruit different effector proteins, which in turn bias transcriptional programmes. This is analogous to the histone code: modifications create binding sites for reader proteins; the functional outcome depends on the combination. CAMC is a **state marker** reflecting centriole history, not a binary lock.

## Box 1. Quantitative Model of Centriolar Tagging

Let *p* be the probability that the older centriole is retained by the stem daughter. The expected centriole age after *n* divisions:

**E[a_n] = p × (1 − pⁿ)/(1 − p)** → **E[a_∞] = p/(1 − p)**

| *p* | E[a_∞] |
|:----|:------:|
| 0.50 | 1.0 (symmetric) |
| 0.70 | 2.3 |
| 0.90 | 9.0 |

With conservative parameters (*f* = 1% of SCs, *p* = 0.51, *k* = 12 divisions/yr, *T* = 70 yr): **N_aged = 0.043 × M** (4.3% of tissue cells). Realistic (*f* = 0.05, *p* = 0.9): **38%.**

**Caveat:** *p* is system-dependent. CCP deglutamylases (rate *k_ccp*) and de novo centriole assembly during meiosis reset the tag. Physical damage (*k_damage*) accumulates independently of PTM state. The model is a first-order approximation; a full model requires *p*, *k_ccp*, *k_damage*, and a commitment threshold *G_crit*.

## 6. Tissues: Modulation vs. True Differentiation

Standard histology (Ham & Cormack; Junqueira & Carneiro) distinguishes two fundamentally different cell states:

- **Modulation** — reversible change in gene expression. One set of genes is transiently up-regulated, another transiently down-regulated. No gene networks are **irreversibly closed.** The cell remains **totipotent** — capable of regenerating the entire organism. This is the state of bacterial biofilm cells, fungal hyphal compartments, and the living cells of plants (mesophyll, cortex, parenchyma). A single *Arabidopsis* mesophyll cell can undergo somatic embryogenesis and regenerate a complete fertile plant — the definitive test of totipotency [40]. **Xylem PCD and phloem nuclear loss are not counterarguments — they are phenoptosis, a consequence of modulation, not evidence of irreversible differentiation. The rule: if a plant cell is alive and nucleated, it is totipotent.** The only exception in plants is germ cells — which exhibit true irreversible differentiation, the basal evolutionary phenomenon. In animals, the opposite holds: a somatic cell can never regenerate an organism (SCNT requires nuclear transfer into an enucleated oocyte). Parthenogenesis is possible from an oocyte, never from a sperm. The discussion here concerns somatic cells.

- **True differentiation** — irreversible closure of certain gene networks and permanent activation of others. The cell is **locked.** It cannot dedifferentiate under physiological conditions. This is the state of animal somatic cells: a neuron cannot become a cardiomyocyte; a keratinocyte cannot become a hepatocyte. Reprogramming (OSKM) demonstrates that the lock is epigenetic — not mutational — but it requires extreme intervention.

Thus, in bacterial colonies, fungal hyphae, and plant tissues, there are **no truly differentiated cells** — only modulated cells. In animal tissues, with the exception of tissue-specific stem cells, cells are **truly differentiated.** The definitive functional test: **a single animal somatic cell cannot regenerate a whole organism** (SCNT required). **A single somatic plant cell that is alive and possesses all organelles can** — directly, via somatic embryogenesis. The exception within animals is instructive: acentriolar blastomeres of the early mammalian embryo are totipotent; laser ablation or PLK4 inhibition that removes centrioles increases developmental plasticity. The centriolar ratchet is proposed as the molecular basis of this fundamental animal-plant distinction.

**Counterexamples and boundary conditions:** (i) **Planarians** maintain pluripotent neoblasts that lack centrioles entirely [41] — consistent with the model: acentriolar cells remain totipotent. Planarian regeneration is not a counterargument but a **validation:** the absence of centrioles correlates with the absence of irreversible differentiation. (ii) **Axolotls** dedifferentiate via blastema formation. The mechanism is unknown, but the model predicts that blastema cells either eliminate centrioles, suppress CAMC, or reactivate a centriole-free state — each of which is testable. (iii) **Nematodes** (*C. elegans*) eliminate centrioles in 88% of embryonic cells, retaining them only in the germline [47] — the most extreme natural experiment in lineage-specific centriole elimination. (iv) Mammalian SC compartments renew without dedifferentiation. These cases define the model's phylogenetic boundaries, not its refutation. **On xylem and neurons:** Xylem PCD is programmed death, not differentiation — a dead cell has no gene networks to lock. The proper comparison is between a living plant parenchyma cell (totipotent) and a living animal neuron (irreversibly differentiated). That distinction is the subject of this essay.

## 7. Oxygen and True Multicellularity

**O₂ as a speculative driver — see Supplementary Discussion.** The GOE (~2.4 Ga) and animal emergence (~0.6 Ga) are separated by ~1.8 Ga. No direct evidence links ROS to centriolar polyGlu. This hypothesis is presented as speculation, not a pillar of the model.

**Alternative commitment mechanisms exist** — DNA methylation, histone modifications, transcriptional networks (the Waddington landscape). The centriolar pathway is not proposed as the sole mechanism but as a **structural complement:** epigenetic modifications lock the genome; the centriole locks the cytoplasm. Both are required for true multicellularity. Somatic plant cells, lacking centrioles, rely entirely on epigenetic and positional mechanisms — which allow totipotency. Animal cells, possessing centrioles, add a cytoplasmic lock — which enables true tissues but at the cost of totipotency.

## 8. Aging: The Price of Irreversibility

If the centriole is a molecular state marker that biases cell fate, then aging is the gradual **failure of that marking system.** This is not an adaptive trait — it is the inevitable consequence of a compensatory mechanism that was optimised for developmental robustness, not for indefinite maintenance.

The CEDAR model [43,44] proposes: polyGlu accumulates over successive divisions — **whether this reflects CCP insufficiency or simply a shift in TTLL/CCP equilibrium is unknown and is a key prediction to be tested.** The PTM platform degrades; asymmetric inheritance fidelity declines; cells stochastically escape commitment — either dedifferentiating (→ cancer) or prematurely locking (→ stem cell exhaustion). [44] is peer-reviewed (*Mol. Biol. Rep.*, PMID 36583780); [43] is a preprint (Zenodo).

**Aging is the gradual failure of that marking system.** This is a testable proposition, not a tautology: it predicts that (i) the rate of polyGlu accumulation per stem cell division correlates with the rate of tissue functional decline across species; (ii) interventions that reduce centriolar polyGlu accumulation (e.g., CCP overexpression, antioxidant targeting to the centrosome) should delay age-associated phenotypes; (iii) tissues with higher stem cell turnover should age faster than those with lower turnover, controlling for other factors. Reversibility via CCP enzymes [24] and other deglutamylases means the system can, in principle, be reset — a potential therapeutic direction.

**Independent convergent evidence — the centromere side of the tagging system.** The tagging system is not limited to the centriole. Sikder et al. (2025) showed that centromeres become inactivated during aging in human cells and that this inactivation can be rescued [52]; Takata et al. (2023) linked centromere-chromatin fluctuation to cellular senescence by CRISPR imaging [53]; and Wu et al. (2023) demonstrated that centrosome amplification drives a HIF-1α-dependent variant SASP, directly connecting centrosome damage to inflammation [54]. If the tagging system fails in concert, aged stem cells should show co-occurring CENP-A loss, centromere de-clustering, and polyGlu drift — a joint prediction testable by Hi-C, CENP-A ChIP-seq, and GT335 immunofluorescence on young versus old tissue (see also P3).

## 9. Alternative Models

**(a) UNIMOD — Universal Module (Carvalho-Santos et al., 2010) [45].** LECA possessed a conserved core of centriolar proteins (SAS-6, SAS-4/CPAP, BLD10/CEP135) that could assemble either into a centriole or an acentriolar MTOC depending on ecological context. UNIMOD dissolves the «invasion» narrative: the centriole is not an external acquisition but a **regulatory switch** — the toolkit was present; the decision to deploy it was ecological. This is compatible with the architectural rule: wall → toolkit suppressed; no wall → toolkit deployed. No HGT or independent origins required.

**(b) Viral eukaryogenesis (Forterre & Gaïa, 2016) [24].** PhuZ is the strongest evidence for a viral contribution: a virus-encoded tubulin forming a dynamic bipolar spindle functionally analogous to the eukaryotic mitotic spindle. The phage nucleus (chimallin) further demonstrates viral self-assembling compartments. Under this model, the centriole, nucleus, and mitotic apparatus derive from viral infection of an archaeal host. **Strengths:** explains functional parallelism without sequence homology. **Weaknesses:** no viral homologs of SAS-6, PLK4, or CEP135 exist.

**(c) Autogenous origin (consensus) [22].** The centriole assembled autogenously within LECA. Supported by centriolar gene homologs across all supergroups.

**(d) Spirochete endosymbiosis (Margulis, 1967) [23].** No molecular evidence. Phylogenomic data place all eukaryotic tubulins within the archaeal FtsZ lineage.

**(e) Centriole as marker, not driver.** An alternative interpretation is that centriole asymmetry is a **consequence**, not a cause, of cell-fate determination. Asymmetric distribution of fate determinants (Numb, Notch, Sara endosomes) may establish cell fate, with the centriole passively inherited along with these determinants. The Bradford-Hill criteria are satisfied by correlation; definitive proof requires showing that experimental reversal of centriole asymmetry reverses cell fate — a prediction tested by P2.

## 10. Predictions

### P1. *Dictyostelium* centriole assembly competence — the diamond experiment

Expression of a full centriolar gene complement — PLK4, SAS-6, CEP135, CPAP, POC1, γ-tubulin, and centrin — in *Dictyostelium* AX2 cells. Fix at 24, 48, 72 h. TEM for 9-fold symmetric structures; immunogold for SAS-6. **Functional test:** if structures form, test for cilium nucleation capacity using an ARL13B-GFP reporter — a bona fide centriole must be able to dock at the membrane and nucleate a cilium. **Positive control:** same construct in *Chlamydomonas* or early mouse embryos. **Controls:** empty vector; subsets of genes. **Prediction:** 9-fold symmetric, cilium-competent structures in the full complement. **Interpretation:** Positive (structure + cilium) → latent assembly capacity retained. Negative → additional factors required.

### P2. De novo centrioles enhance reprogramming — redesigned

Primary MEFs with Centrin-GFP. **Condition A:** Centrinone (PLK4 inhibitor, 1 μM, 8 days) — centrioles diluted; tags preserved. **Condition B:** Centrinone washout at day 8 → centrioles reform de novo without age-asymmetric tags. Both receive lentiviral OSKM. **Specificity control:** a kinase inhibitor that does not affect centrioles (e.g., a MEK inhibitor), to rule out generic drug effects on reprogramming. **Cell death control:** Annexin V/PI at day 3. **Prediction:** Condition B > Condition A > DMSO + OSKM.

### P3. Centriole tag burden and asymmetric division — causal test

Isolate HSCs from young (3 mo) and old (24 mo) C57BL/6 mice. Label with H2B-GFP. Live-cell imaging of first division. **Prospectively** track daughter pairs; assign fates by colony assay. Fix and perform STED-GT335. **Correlation prediction:** Older HSCs show elevated GT335 AND reduced asymmetric division fidelity. **Causal test:** CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of TTLL5 (polyglutamylase) or CCP5 (deglutamylase) in HSCs, then repeat the division tracking. If reducing polyGlu (TTLL5-KO) restores asymmetric division in old HSCs, and increasing polyGlu (CCP5-KO) reduces it in young HSCs, the causal chain is established.

### Acknowledged limitations

The PhuZ genomic search (BLASTp/HHpred, §4) was negative, consistent with convergent evolution of dynamic spindle properties. This is reported transparently in §4.

## 11. Concluding Remarks

This essay does not seek to overturn the consensus, which holds that LECA possessed centrioles and is well supported by phylogenomics. It asks different questions. Why can *Dictyostelium* build a centrosome without centrioles? Why are plant somatic cells totipotent while animal somatic cells are not? Why do phages encode tubulins that form dynamic bipolar spindles? Why does centriole amplification cause cancer?

The hypothesis explored here — walled acentriolar bacteria, wall loss in water, the PhuZ paradigm, centriolar tagging as a cell-fate marker — is speculative. It is not a replacement for the consensus but a framework that makes testable predictions and points to anomalies.

Whether the centriole came from a phage or arose autogenously, its role as a molecular tag of cellular history — and the consequences of its failure — are questions that experimental cell biology can now address.
